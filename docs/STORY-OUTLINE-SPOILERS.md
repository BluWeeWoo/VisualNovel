# Developer outline — full-summer spoilers

Do not link this document from the player interface. The local server deliberately does not serve documentation.

## Emotional premise

The protagonist (23) returns to Seabright for six weeks to close Summerhouse, the guesthouse where their grandmother helped raise them. Rowan (23, he/him), the friend next door, remained nearby and helped maintain it. Both miss the same woman while knowing different versions of her final years. Closing the house is not a failure that romance reverses. The sale proceeds in every ending.

The emotional question: can they choose a relationship that makes room for who they have become, rather than trying to resume the one they left behind? Friendship is a complete outcome. Romance adds chosen intimacy, not a superior ending or a cure for grief.

## Rowan

- **Daily life:** works at the chandlery, repairs small things, lives next door, builds an acoustic guitar at a cluttered workbench.
- **Hope:** take an instrument-making course up north next year. Make something that can leave town without needing him. Eventually make an independent home.
- **Voice:** short, concrete humor; affection through preparation; awkward when receiving sincere thanks. Jokes about objects and logistics, not grief. Can let a pause stand.
- **Flaws:** turns being useful into an identity; quietly resents unasked-for obligations; procrastinates on applications; sometimes assumes care means predicting what others need. Needs to ask instead.
- **History:** home next door could be loud and difficult. Grandmother offered food and welcome before explanations. It was not uniformly miserable, and Rowan had friends, work, music, and ambitions outside the guesthouse.
- **Unspoken hurt:** protagonist heard “everything is fine” and believed it. Rowan sometimes wished they would ask a second question, yet also chose not to say how tiring things had become. Neither bears a single simple blame.
- **Boundaries:** accepts space, no hugs, friendship, changed memories, skipped chats, and respectful disagreement. Does not promise forever through chat. No escalating intimacy without authored, explicit invitation.
- **Design:** the user’s official brown-haired, blue-eyed Rowan with a low tied bun, earrings, gray patterned cardigan, and pale shirt. Seven expression variants and an illustrated-book pose retain that design.

## Shared timeline

Both are 23 now. They wrote the five promises at 12. The protagonist moved away at 15, eight years before this summer. Rowan’s notebook has intermittent entries across those eight years; he had his own life and did not spend them waiting. The grandmother died before the current summer. Her February voicemail and Rowan’s recent maintenance work remain consistent.

## The summer, in five chapters

### 1. The house with the blue door — sunrise, begun (implemented)

Arrival and negotiated reunion; one room sorted together. Player establishes personality, headquarters, treasured object, and childhood nickname. Find the five promises. A chip-shop outing restores ordinary chemistry; optional pier walk reveals the grandmother’s reluctant role as a musical audience. A third cup makes her absence tangible. Plan 4:40 with tea, coffee, or cocoa. Exchange numbers and set a friendship/open/undecided tone. Four short text exchanges preview the eventual phone system; skipping is equally valid.

**Hook:** the protagonist finds Rowan’s notebook, *Things Worth Telling {name}*. Rowan has kept his smaller childhood promise to “save you a bit” of home, over years and with gaps. Not a shrine or evidence that he waited without a life: observations include town changes, jokes, and ordinary days. The player may thank him in person or begin a reciprocal page.

### 2. Four chords — perform a song (outlined)

Open at sunrise, paying off chapter one’s plan and drink ritual. If plans need to change, another morning remains valid. The notebook is discussed without making its years a debt. Later, sort the sitting room and find the grandmother’s battered songbook. Rowan plays while the protagonist hums, accompanies, or simply listens. The promise can be fulfilled with one trusted audience member, no forced public performance. A playful rehearsal becomes the first short Trusted text window.

Key choice: ask what kind of help Rowan wants, or acknowledge having jumped in too quickly. Outcome quality follows negotiated care, not whether the protagonist is talented or extroverted. Remember their chosen childhood headquarters in rehearsal banter.

### 3. Things we didn’t say — tell a secret (outlined)

The lemon recipe leads to an ordinary cooking scene. The protagonist chooses whether and when to play the grandmother’s saved voicemail; waiting remains valid. Her message concerns a missing baking tin and the weather, not a posthumous command to fall in love. Rowan reveals resentment about being indispensable, alongside love for the woman who welcomed him. Protagonist may articulate guilt, hurt at being excluded, or difficulty facing decline.

A secret is voluntarily offered; the list is never used to demand disclosure. Authored boundaries and mutual repair establish Trusted. Close unlocks only after this shared conversation, whether platonic or romantic. AI chats can discuss known feelings, but cannot invent the secret or perform its reconciliation.

### 4. Anywhere, within reason — no planned destination (outlined)

Take a bus at random within a jointly agreed time/budget limit. A wrong stop, closed café, and improvised picnic let humor coexist with mourning. Rowan finally articulates his desire to apply for the course. The protagonist must resist equating helping him leave with saving him.

Authored branch: explicit romantic interest with consent, chosen close friendship, or continued uncertainty. An evening near the beach permits a romantic scene only on the mutual-interest path. Either relationship supports the application. An offered practical ritual (quiet application hour, exchanged music, weekly postcard) is chosen rather than imposed.

### 5. The gate swings quietly — leave town together someday (outlined)

Final guesthouse sorting. The unfinished floorboard is repaired cooperatively or left honestly on the handover list. Keep some things and let others go. The player negotiates what the last promise means in their current lives: share the first leg of a journey, choose distance with a realistic plan, or lovingly retire the literal childhood promise.

Rowan decides his application independently; the protagonist may support or ask for clarity but cannot submit it for him. The house closes. The last scene revisits the now-quiet gate, with a cup, drink, photograph, or small approved detail carried forward.

## Trust, without an affection meter

| Milestone | Authored condition and unlocked behavior |
| --- | --- |
| Reacquainted | Reunion establishes boundaries, present tone, and flexible childhood memory. |
| Familiar | Sharing the sorting and lunch gives room for optional questions and banter. The opening includes a limited four-message preview, explicitly an exception rather than Trusted/Open chat. |
| Trusted | Later authored honesty and respectful response open short texting windows. Disagreement can demonstrate care; agreement is not rewarded by default. |
| Close | Reciprocal vulnerability with space for repair opens between-chapter AI conversations, still chapter-bounded. |
| Committed | An explicit mutual choice of a durable friendship or romantic relationship. Rituals are negotiated. Romance has no monopoly on commitment. |

Chapter one implements only Reacquainted → Familiar. Later milestones are defined but cannot be earned by grinding the opening chat. No score decreases for time away, declining romance, or skipping optional scenes.

Internally retain categorical facts, not a summed “goodness” score: reunion boundary; company/humor/independence; chosen memories; regret/curiosity/honest disagreement; listening/challenging/practical support; grief preference; drink ritual; friendship/open/undecided; notebook response. Later chapters should retain whether help was requested, whether a boundary was understood or repaired, and whether future plans are explicit. Avoid counting “correct” choices.

## Endings

**Hopeful — A place in each other’s days.** Both make an explicit, realistic plan they can revise. They take the first leg of a journey together or choose distance with a ritual they both want. Rowan pursues the course for himself. Friendship: an equally full final scene and epilogue. Romance: chosen intimacy and practical distance/travel plans, without assuming cohabitation. The saved voicemail can still remain unplayed. Hope does not require completing every promise or removing sadness.

**Bittersweet — The summer we got back.** Both acknowledge that their immediate futures will not align, or the protagonist explicitly declines an ongoing commitment. They keep the restored connection and its limits without extracting another promise. Rowan still pursues an independent future. A letter months later contains a small ordinary event, with no guilt or demand to answer. This is shaped by explicit end-of-summer choices, not skipped texting, low affection, or failure to romance.

Future ending selection must be authored and preview the implications of the final commitment decision. No hidden failure ending. No surprise illness, no shock death, and no rescue of the guesthouse by sudden money.
